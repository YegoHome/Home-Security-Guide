Your Home Security Guide

Part 1: The Security Walk

Think of your computer as your home. Just like you check your doors and windows before going to sleep, we're going to take a walk around your digital home.

In this part, we change nothing. We only look.

About 15 minutesNo administrator rights needed

This is an experimental learning guide. If you apply any step, you do so at your own risk. Please read the Disclaimer first.

How Your Digital Home Works

Programs on your computer open numbered doors (called ports) so other devices can talk to them. Door 445, for example, is used for Windows file sharing.

The house metaphor
  • An open door (LISTENING) is open and waiting. Anyone who can reach it can walk up and try to come in. Nobody needs to knock.
  • An inside door only connects rooms inside your home. Other devices can't reach it.
  • A door on every side faces every network your computer is on. Only your locks decide who gets through.

Today's goal

Make a list of the open doors, who opened each one, and which side of the house it faces. In Part 2, you'll use that list to decide which doors to lock.

Seeing open doors is normal. Many are part of Windows itself. The goal is to spot the ones that shouldn't be reachable from other devices.

Step 1: Finding the Right Tool

Windows has a built-in program called PowerShell. In Windows 11, it usually opens inside an app called Terminal. It looks like a dark window with text, but it's easier than it appears.

  1. Find the Start button (the Windows logo) at the bottom of your screen.
  2. Right-click it (or press Windows + X).
  3. A menu appears. Look for Terminal or Windows PowerShell.

Which option should you choose?

What you seeLike having…For Part 1
Terminal or Windows PowerShellA visitor pass: you can walk around, look, and take notesChoose this one
Terminal (Admin) or Windows PowerShell (Admin)The master keys: full access to change locks and settingsSkip for now

Windows protects itself from accidental changes. If something needs the master keys, you'll see an error like "Access is denied." That's a safety feature, not a bug.

Today we're only looking, so the visitor pass is the right choice.

Step 2: Starting the Walk

When the window opens, you'll see a blinking cursor and a line like this:

PS C:\Users\YourName>

This is your Command Center. It's waiting for you to type an instruction.

Getting comfortable

  • Click once inside the window so the cursor is active.
  • Type or paste a command, then press Enter.
  • To paste, right-click inside the window or press Ctrl + V.
  • Every command in Part 1 only reads information. None of them change anything.
Tip: every dark gray command box in this guide has a Copy button. Copying avoids typing mistakes.

Step 3: Checking for Open Doors

Ask your computer to list every connection and every open door:

netstat -ano

A long list scrolls by. Don't worry, we're only looking for patterns. According to Microsoft's netstat reference, -a shows connections and listening ports, -n shows numbers instead of names, and -o adds the process ID (PID) of the program that owns each line.

The Decoder Ring

Word you seeIn the houseWhat it means
LISTENINGAn open door, waiting for visitorsA program is ready to accept a connection
ESTABLISHEDA conversation happening through a door right nowYour computer is actively talking to another device
TIME_WAITA conversation just ended; the door is closing slowlyThe connection is finished and Windows is tidying up
CLOSE_WAITThe visitor left, but you're still holding the doorThe other side ended; this computer hasn't fully let go yet

Cleaner view: open doors only

netstat -ano | findstr LISTENING

Step 4: Which Side of the House?

Look at the Local Address column. The part before the colon shows which side of the house the door faces. The number after the colon is the door number.

  Proto  Local Address        Foreign Address      State        PID
  TCP    0.0.0.0:135          0.0.0.0:0            LISTENING    1052
  TCP    0.0.0.0:445          0.0.0.0:0            LISTENING    4
  TCP    127.0.0.1:5354       0.0.0.0:0            LISTENING    3920
  TCP    192.168.X.X:139      0.0.0.0:0            LISTENING    4
Local Address starts withIn the houseWho could reach it
0.0.0.0 or [::]Door on every sideDevices on any network this computer is connected to, unless a lock stops them
127.0.0.1 or [::1]Inside doorOnly programs on this same computer
A specific address, such as 192.168.X.XA door on one sideDevices on the network that address belongs to
What to write down: every LISTENING line whose address starts with 0.0.0.0, [::], or a specific address. Inside doors (127.0.0.1, [::1]) can't be reached from other devices.

Step 5: Who Opened This Door?

netstat shows a PID, which is just a number. This command lists open doors with the program name next to each one:

Get-NetTCPConnection -State Listen |
  Sort-Object LocalPort |
  Select-Object LocalAddress, LocalPort, OwningProcess,
    @{Name='Program'; Expression={ (Get-Process -Id $_.OwningProcess -ErrorAction SilentlyContinue).ProcessName }} |
  Format-Table -AutoSize

Reading the Program column

  • System (PID 4) and svchost are parts of Windows itself. svchost runs many Windows services, so one name can cover several doors.
  • A program you recognize, such as a game launcher or chat app, is usually expected.
  • A name you don't recognize goes on your list to look up. It isn't automatically dangerous.

Looking up an unknown program

  1. Press Ctrl + Shift + Esc to open Task Manager.
  2. Select Details, then find the matching number in the PID column.
  3. Right-click it and choose Open file location or Search online.

Optional: mail slots (UDP)

Some openings are mail slots. They accept messages without starting a conversation, so they never say LISTENING. To see them:

Get-NetUDPEndpoint | Sort-Object LocalPort |
  Select-Object LocalAddress, LocalPort, OwningProcess,
    @{Name='Program'; Expression={ (Get-Process -Id $_.OwningProcess -ErrorAction SilentlyContinue).ProcessName }} |
  Format-Table -AutoSize

Usually normal

  • Inside doors on 127.0.0.1 or [::1]

Doors 135, 139 and 445 are often targeted by attackers when other devices can reach them. Seeing them here doesn't mean anything is wrong. It means they're worth a lock (Part 2).

Step 7: Taking Notes

Saving what you found helps you compare later. Part 2 starts from these notes.

  1. Click and drag to highlight the text in the window.
  2. Press Ctrl + C (or right-click and choose Copy).
  3. Open Notepad from the Start menu.
  4. Press Ctrl + V to paste.
  5. Save the file as Security_Walk_Notes.txt on your Desktop.

Good habit: keep this first file. In Part 5, your regular patrol will save dated logbooks you can compare with it.

Step 8: Closing the Gate

This is the most important habit in the whole guide.

exit

Type exit and press Enter. The window disappears.

Why we close it

When you finish walking around the house, you don't leave the yard gate open. An open PowerShell window is like that gate:

  • Anyone who sits at your computer could type commands.
  • An accidental keystroke could run something you didn't mean to.
  • There's no reason to leave it open once you're done.

From now on, every part ends by closing the gate, whether you used the visitor pass or the master keys.
